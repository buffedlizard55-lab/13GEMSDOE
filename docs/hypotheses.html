Candidate hypotheses

Every one of these targets a systematic blind spot of a scarp-derived catalogue rather than re-detecting the topographic signature the catalogue already encodes. That is the point: the test labels are, by definition, faults the USGS/INGENIOUS compilation does not contain.

Full write-up with references: knowledge/03_hypotheses.md.

Why not more topography? The USGS Quaternary Fault and Fold Database is built predominantly from surface scarp expression. A better topographic ridge detector finds more of what is already mapped. The organizers confirmed “new fault” means “any fault pixel not already captured”, explicitly including extensions, splays and corrections — so the productive search is for faults whose surface expression is weak, absent, or non-topographic.

Multiscale potential-field “worms”
worst 0.0198 · best 0.0330		 rtp (2), iso_grav_anom (13) Fourier upward continuation to 0.5–8 km, horizontal-gradient maxima at each height, thinned and stacked by cross-scale persistence. A steeply-dipping, vertically-extensive contact keeps producing a gradient maximum as the field is continued upward; shallow sedimentary texture washes out within a few hundred metres. QFaults is built from surface scarp evidence, so it cannot contain contacts buried under basin fill. The repo used the provided single-scale gradient bands (tmi_hg, iso_grav_anom_hg) and generic ridge filters. Explicit upward continuation and cross-scale persistence were never computed. High

H-B
Tilt-derivative zero contour / theta map
worst 0.0274 · best 0.0515		 rtp (2), iso_grav_anom (13) TDR = atan2(dF/dz, |∇ₕF|); the zero contour sits over the source edge. Also θ = acos(THDR/AS). TDR is amplitude-normalised, so a weak anomaly over a deep or low-contrast source scores as strongly as a loud shallow one. Loud anomalies are already mapped; the unmapped ones are quiet by construction. Nothing in the repo computes a vertical derivative or a tilt angle from RTP. Band 6 tc was assumed to be a tilt angle, but it is almost certainly radiometric Total Count (irregularity I-2). High
cost Low
H-C
Concealed basement hinge under flat cover
worst 0.0172 · best 0.0381		 depth_to_base_surf (15), det_elev_slope (19) Ridge of |∇ depth-to-basement|, thinned, then multiplied by a flat-topography mask (bottom ~55% of slope). A normal fault that offsets basement but is buried by Quaternary fill makes a hinge in basement depth and nothing at the surface. Conditioning on flat ground inverts the usual scarp hunt so the detector can only fire where a scarp-derived catalogue is blind. This is also the classic hidden-geothermal target that motivates INGENIOUS. Conductivity and depth-to-basement were used as generic ML features. The anti-topographic conjunction — require a basement step AND no relief — is new. Medium-High
cost Low

Strain-budget residual
worst 0.0108 · best 0.0760		 geod_2ndinv (4), ieq_n100a15 (16), + visible catalogue density Normalised second invariant of the strain-rate tensor minus normalised earthquake density minus normalised mapped-fault density. Geodetic strain has to be accommodated somewhere. Where strain is high but both seismicity and mapped faults are absent, the accommodating structure is by elimination not in the catalogue. Strain bands were fed to ML models and a “dilational annulus/top-k” was tried, but never as a deficit conditioned on catalogue density. Low-Medium

Honest limitation: none of the five hypotheses above requires external data — all five run on the 19 official bands, which is deliberate. The external sources would strengthen the holdout (age/slip-rate strata) and add a genuinely new geothermal channel (paleo-hydrothermal deposits), not the detectors.
